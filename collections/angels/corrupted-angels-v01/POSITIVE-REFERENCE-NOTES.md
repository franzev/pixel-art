## What “not overly done” means in these references

### Clear hierarchy

Each angel has one immediate read:

- a disciplined armored spear bearer;
- a solemn veiled vessel bearer;
- a hooded lantern pilgrim.

The identity does not require many competing symbols, ornaments, weapons, or
magical effects. The face or head treatment, body, wings, and primary object
remain easy to find.

### Controlled silhouettes

All three use a coherent full-body silhouette with two readable wings and a
small halo. Their shapes differ without becoming chaotic:

- the first is upright, armored, and symmetrical;
- the second is a narrow, quiet vertical column;
- the third is an asymmetrical walking-pilgrim shape.

No oversized spell circle, floating debris field, excessive wing count, or
decorative appendage competes with the figure.

### Purposeful costume construction

The approved examples clarify that robes are not inherently a problem.

- The first uses compact plate armor with a controlled number of shapes.
- The second uses a long veil and robe that read as one unified garment system.
- The third uses a hooded cloak or tunic with practical travel layers.

Avoiding “overdone” clothing means avoiding unexplained stacks of fabric,
repeated skirts, cascading panels, excessive trim, and ornament added only to
fill space. It does not mean every angel should be bare-chested or dressed in
shorts.

### Restrained equipment

Equipment is limited and role-defining:

- one spear;
- one small blue vessel held with both hands;
- one staff and one lantern that belong to the same pilgrim role.

Props support posture and identity rather than functioning as isolated visual
gimmicks.

### Restrained palette and accents

The shared foundation is warm near-black, dirty ivory, ash grey, dark metal,
and muted gold. Each design uses very limited accents, such as the blue vessel
or brass lantern. Accent color does not spread across every garment and effect.

### Pixel-scale readability

The figures remain legible at 256 × 256 because the large value groups are
clear. Fine detail stays inside strong armor, robe, wing, and equipment shapes
instead of replacing those shapes with texture.

## How to apply these anchors to corrupted angels

Start with the same compositional restraint, then add corruption deliberately:

1. Preserve one clear role and one readable primary silhouette.
2. Use one primary corruption event, such as a wounded wing, fused relic,
   blackened vein network, molten armor break, or controlled exposed-flesh area.
3. Add at most one secondary magical accent, such as a broken halo, compact
   sigil, a few embers, or a restrained blood effect.
4. Keep the corruption physically integrated with the body, clothing, wings,
   armor, or carried object.
5. Stop adding detail when the role, corruption, and silhouette are already
   clear.

## Positive-anchor checklist

Before retaining a new angel, compare it directly with all three approved
crops:

- Can its role be understood in one short phrase?
- Is the main silhouette as clear as these three?
- Does the clothing read as one coherent outfit?
- Are garment layers functional and countable rather than decorative noise?
- Does it avoid both excessive robes and the bare-torso-with-shorts default?
- Is there one primary prop or one cohesive equipment set?
- Are halo, magic, gore, and corruption secondary to the figure?
- Is the palette restrained, with no more than one or two accent colors?
- Does it remain clear at 256 × 256?

If the answer to several questions is no, the concept has become overly done
and should be simplified before more images are generated.

## Reference-use rule

When supplying visual references to an image-generation workflow, use the three
individual approved crops. Do not use the full eight-character sheet without
explicitly stating that only columns one through three are positive anchors.
